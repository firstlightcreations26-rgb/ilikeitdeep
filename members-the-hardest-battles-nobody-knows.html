The Hardest Battles Nobody Knows

— S, founder of I Like It Deep

I've been watching this Douglas Murray investigation on YouTube about drugs, and it wrecked me. 💔 It's so sad, and it hits home a little too well. Addiction isn't a choice — no matter what most people want to believe. We didn't ask for our brains to be wired different. We don't enjoy ruining our lives. We don't like having an addiction. My whole life I was anti-drug. I didn't touch anything until I was 30 years old. I'm not going to get into all of it here, because it breaks my heart daily to think about what I lost. There are days the weight of it is almost unbearable. It's terrifying how something can take over your entire life. I never in a million years thought mine would take a turn this dark.

Before I open up about the drugs, I think you need to know where I come from — maybe then some of this makes sense. So let's start there. I've always felt like the black sheep. I was adopted at nine months old (my parents paid five grand for me — I used to joke they picked the wrong puppy). I don't remember most of my childhood, or most of my birthdays. What I do remember is my grandpa Wayne. He built our family cabin, and he was my favorite person in the entire world. He always had my back. He'd take me out with him while my sister stayed with grandma. I was Wayne's girl.

The house I grew up in was strict — uptight, “Christian,” money-hungry, judgmental. Good people on paper. We ate dinner at the table every night with Friends or the news on. We did homework on the kitchen counter every day after school, and I was always stuck there longer than my little sister, because nothing ever stuck for me. I had ADD. I wasn't dumb — I just couldn't make myself care about things that didn't interest me. (Ask me anything about dogs and I've got you.) From 4th grade until I graduated, I was on medication for it. To this day I can't stand taking any meds, because of how that felt as a kid.

I never really got along with my mom. She had a way of making me feel like I didn't belong. She'd say things in front of my friends like “why can't you be smart like Alicia?” I got called dumb, hard to handle, talked down to constantly. And there was this thing she did — she'd fake-cry the second she heard the garage door, right as my dad got home. He'd walk in already angry because his wife was upset, and somehow it always ended with me catching hell downstairs over something I didn't even do. I still don't understand it. You have to be a special kind of broken to make your adopted kid the reason for all your stress. I used to tell her: God said no from the beginning. Seven miscarriages meant no.

You take a kid who already feels like she got picked wrong, and you drop her into a house where you have to look and act a certain way at all times — and this is what you get. Someone awkward with affection. Someone who doesn't trust anyone. Someone who never feels like enough, and who's boy-crazy because at least attention felt like being wanted. And I got exposed to way too much, way too young — pulled into things I didn't understand and had no business being anywhere near, before anyone ever taught me what any of it meant. Nobody protected me from it, and nobody handled it with anything close to care. When my mom found out something had happened, her big fix was hauling me to church to become a “born-again virgin.” 😂 As if that undoes anything. That was the start of years of treating sex like it was nothing — like a handshake, something I just handed out — because it never got to be something sacred or safe for me in the first place. That's an addiction that started long before any drug did, and I still wrestle with it today.

Here's the part that still gets me: my biggest regret was asking for help. I genuinely thought that's what you're supposed to do when you want to quit — you reach out. But growing up, “help” in my house looked like getting shipped off to Hazelden at sixteen for smoking pot one time. One time. So somewhere in me, asking for help and getting punished got wired together. That is a hard thing to untangle when your life actually depends on reaching out.

I'm not telling you all this for sympathy. I'm telling you because when you see someone struggling — with drugs, with sex, with just holding it together — you're seeing the very end of a long story you know nothing about. The hardest battles really are the ones nobody knows. I'm still in mine. But I'm here, I'm in treatment, and I'm still writing it down. That has to count for something.
